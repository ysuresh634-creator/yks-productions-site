01

What a comp card is

A comp card — short for composite card, and also called a zed card or Z-card — is a one-sheet summary of a model. The front carries your strongest headshot and your name. The back carries a small selection of images that show range, along with your measurements and how to reach you. It is the page a casting team keeps on file, forwards to a client, or lays on a table next to other options.

Where your portfolio is the full story, the comp card is the summary. It has to work in a few seconds, at a glance, often on a phone screen. That’s why the rules are strict: few images, clean layout, accurate numbers.

02

Comp card sizes: A5 and 5.5 × 8.5 in

There are two sizes you’ll see most often:

  • A5 — 148 × 210 mm. The international paper standard, and the most practical choice in India because any print shop can produce it.
  • 5.5 × 8.5 inches (about 140 × 216 mm). Half of a US letter sheet; common in the US and with international clients.

Both are portrait, both are double-sided, and they’re close enough that a well-designed card works at either. The maker above exports both. If you’re unsure, choose A5.

For print, the file should be high resolution — the maker renders each side at 300 pixels per inch, which is the usual standard for sharp print. Ask your printer whether they need bleed (a few extra millimetres around the edge that gets trimmed); if they do, they’ll usually tell you exactly how much.

03

Which stats to include

Every number on a comp card is something a client may plan a fitting or a costume around, so accuracy matters far more than flattery. Include:

  • Height — in feet and inches and in centimetres. Both are used in India; international clients usually prefer centimetres.
  • Bust, waist and hips — measured with a soft tape, standing relaxed, over light clothing. Inches are the norm in most Indian studios.
  • Shoe size — state the system, e.g. UK or EU, so there’s no guessing.
  • Hair and eye colour — simple, honest words: black, dark brown, brown, hazel.
  • City — where you’re based, so clients know whether travel is involved.
  • A contact route — a professional email or your Instagram handle is enough.

Leave off your date of birth, home address and personal phone number. They aren’t needed to assess you for a shoot, and a comp card gets forwarded far beyond the person you first sent it to. Dress size is optional; if you include it, state the system.

04

Layout: what goes where

The front

One image: your best, clearest headshot — face to camera or a soft three-quarter, natural expression, minimal styling. Your name in large, clean type. Nothing else competes with it. The front is how you’re remembered, so choose the image where you look most like yourself on a good day.

The back

Two to four images, each answering a different question:

  1. A full-length shot — proportion, posture, how clothes hang.
  2. A commercial smile — warm, approachable, believable.
  3. An editorial or fashion frame — mood and styling.
  4. A look for your market — for many Indian briefs that’s an ethnic, festive or bridal look; for beauty, a clean close-up.

Then your stats in a single, tidy band, and your name and contact again in small type. Don’t repeat the front image on the back, and don’t use four versions of the same pose — that’s range lost.

Typography and colour

Keep it quiet. One typeface for your name, one for the stats, lots of space, and a plain background. The pictures do the work; decoration only distracts. The three layouts in the maker — Classic, Editorial and Minimal — all follow these rules in slightly different ways.

05

Digital vs print

Most comp cards in India today are shared digitally — as a PDF or an image on WhatsApp or email — rather than handed over in person. You’ll still want both versions:

  • PNG — front and back side by side in one image. Best for WhatsApp, Instagram DMs and quick sharing.
  • PDF — two pages, front and back, at print size. Best for email, for clients who keep files, and for taking to a printer.

If you print, a matte or silk card stock usually looks more premium than gloss, which shows fingerprints and glare. Print a small number — your look and measurements will change, and an out-of-date card does more harm than none. Keep a fresh set of digitals alongside the card; a client who likes your card will often ask for them next.

06

Comp card tips for India

  • Give height both ways. 5’7″ / 170 cm covers every client.
  • Match the back to the work you want. Ethnic wear, jewellery and bridal are large categories in India; if that’s your direction, show one strong look in it.
  • Keep the file light enough to send. If a PDF is too heavy for a client’s inbox, share the PNG instead.
  • Use the name you work under, consistently, across the card, Instagram and any portfolio.
  • Be cautious who you send it to. A comp card is personal information. Send it to verified clients and businesses — read the fees and scams guide if anything feels off.
  • Never pay for a “compulsory” comp card package as a condition of being considered. You can make a good one here, free.
